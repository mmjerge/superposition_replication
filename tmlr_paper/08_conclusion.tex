\section{Conclusion}

This paper has replicated and extended the foundational study of superposition in neural networks by \citet{elhage2022toy}. Our independent implementation confirms their core findings: superposition emerges in autoencoders when features are sparse and dimensions are constrained, with features arranging into geometric configurations (pentagons, hexagons) that minimize interference. We further demonstrate that these patterns are not artifacts of toy settings. GPT-2-style transformers exhibit the same geometric feature organization and sparsity-dependent polysemanticity, a translation model maintains performance despite 32$\times$ dimensional compression, and a computation model performs nonlinear transformations on superposed features through a 3.3$\times$ bottleneck. Across all architectures, superposition emerges whenever $n > d$, with compression ratio governing interference structure (Table~\ref{tab:full_comparison}).

Motivated by this consistency, we proposed a framework connecting representational superposition with reasoning superposition in continuous chain-of-thought models. Controlled experiments comparing single-step and iterative models under matched compression validate this connection: Coconut achieves lower interference than Continuous Thought at every bottleneck dimension, with CO-$d128$ outperforming CT-$d256$ despite using half the dimensions. However, the effective capacity multiplier from iterative reasoning ($\sim$2--4$\times$) falls short of the theoretical prediction, and learned representations remain well above the Welch bound, reflecting the gap between theoretical optimality and practical task-driven optimization.

These findings have implications for mechanistic interpretability and AI safety. The universality of superposition across architectures suggests that interpretability techniques developed on simple models should transfer to practical systems, though disentangling superposed features remains a significant challenge. Future work should test these findings at larger scales and investigate whether the representational-reasoning connection extends to other forms of latent computation.
