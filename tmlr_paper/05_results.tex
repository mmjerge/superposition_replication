\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Transformer Results}

The GPT-2-style transformer exhibits clear superposition patterns when processing synthetic sparse features.

\paragraph{Feature geometry (Figure~\ref{fig:transformer_features}).} We visualize the 128 feature directions from the input projection layer, projected onto two hidden dimensions. Features arrange themselves with approximately uniform angular spacing---the same geometric organization observed in toy autoencoders. This suggests the transformer has learned to minimize interference by distributing features evenly in the representational space.

\paragraph{Effect of sparsity (Figure~\ref{fig:feature_analysis}).} The upper panels show feature norms (bar height) and polysemanticity (color intensity, green=low, yellow=high) for models trained with different sparsity levels. At low sparsity (0.070, features frequently active), the first $\sim$64 features (up to the hidden dimension, marked by dashed line) have high norms and low polysemanticity---they occupy dedicated dimensions. Features beyond this boundary have lower norms and higher polysemanticity, indicating superposition. At high sparsity (0.684, features rarely active), more features participate in superposition, with polysemanticity spread more evenly across features. The model exploits sparsity to represent more features.

The lower panels show weight correlation matrices ($\mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{W}$), revealing feature interactions. Higher sparsity produces denser correlation patterns, indicating more feature sharing.

\paragraph{Dimensional fractions (Figure~\ref{fig:geometric_analysis}).} We analyze what fraction of a dimension each feature occupies. Features cluster around theoretically significant fractions ($1/2$, $2/3$, $3/4$), as predicted by the original paper's analysis of optimal feature packing. This clustering becomes more pronounced at higher inverse sparsity (more superposition), suggesting systematic organization rather than random interference.

\subsection{Translation Model Results}

The bottleneck translation model demonstrates that superposition enables practical performance under severe capacity constraints.

\paragraph{Translation quality.} Despite compressing 512-dimensional encoder outputs to just 16 dimensions, the model achieves a BLEU score of 36.34 on newsdiscussdev2015-enfr, compared to 33.8 for the baseline model without bottleneck. The bottleneck did not impair---and slightly improved---translation quality. We attribute this modest improvement to a regularization effect: the information bottleneck forces the model to discard noise and retain only the most task-relevant features, similar to the compression benefit observed in variational and information-bottleneck methods \citep{tishby2000information}.

\paragraph{Weight distribution.} The bottleneck layer weights exhibit a peaked distribution with symmetric tails, indicating the model learned structured, efficient encoding rather than random projections.

\paragraph{Embedding space visualization (Figure~\ref{fig:translation_embeddings}).} t-SNE visualization of the 256-dimensional bottleneck space reveals strong clustering by part-of-speech category. Nouns (pink), verbs (orange), adjectives (gold), adpositions (teal), conjunctions (blue), and pronouns (purple) each form distinct regions, with numerals and punctuation isolated at the periphery. This POS-structure emerges without any syntactic supervision, driven entirely by the next-token prediction objective under bottleneck compression.

This organization is theoretically significant: part-of-speech categories define a natural sparsity structure, since tokens of the same POS rarely co-occur in the same syntactic slot. The bottleneck space thus learns to exploit this sparsity, consistent with the prediction that $P_{ij} \approx 0$ for tokens of distinct POS categories, enabling their superposition in the same dimensions.

\subsection{Sparsity Governs Interference: Validating the Unified Loss}

Figure~\ref{fig:interference_sparsity} shows the Gram matrix $G_{ij} = \langle \mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{w}_j \rangle$ at two extremes of feature activation probability. At $p = 1.0$ (no sparsity, $S = 0$), the off-diagonal entries span the full range $[-1, 1]$, with features forced into strongly correlated or anti-correlated encodings. At $p = 0.05$ (high sparsity, $S \approx 0.998$), the same $d$-dimensional model encodes the same $n$ features with substantially reduced and more structured interference.

This is the central empirical prediction of Theorem~\ref{thm:equivalence}: because $\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} = \sum_{i \neq j} P_{ij} G_{ij}^2$ and $P_{ij} = p^2$ in the representational setting, reducing $p$ directly reduces the co-occurrence weight on each interfering pair. The model responds by finding encodings where $G_{ij}^2$ can be nonzero for more pairs, representing more features in superposition at the same interference budget $\tau$.

\subsection{Representational and Reasoning Models Converge to the Same Geometry}

Figure~\ref{fig:interference_comparison} shows interference heatmaps for the \texttt{continuous\_thought} and \texttt{coconut} models, both at $d = 256$. The two heatmaps are visually indistinguishable: both show near-zero off-diagonal cosine similarity across all feature pairs, with the diagonal at 1.0.

This convergence is the core empirical evidence for Theorem~\ref{thm:equivalence}. Despite being trained on qualitatively different objectives---feature reconstruction vs.\ next-token prediction with continuous thought---both models arrive at near-orthogonal feature geometries under the same bottleneck dimension. The unified loss $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}, P)$ predicts exactly this: optimal encodings minimize $\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}$ subject to the capacity constraint, and the resulting geometry depends on $d$ and $S(P)$, not on the surface form of the training task.

\subsection{Superposition Emerges Gradually During Training}

Figure~\ref{fig:weight_progression} shows the evolution of feature weight vectors across training, from random initialization (step 0) to convergence (step 14,900). At initialization, features cluster near the origin with small, unsystematic directions. By step 5,000, features begin spreading toward unit norm. By step 14,900, features are distributed with approximately uniform angular spacing---the equiangular structure predicted by the Welch bound (Theorem~\ref{thm:equivalence}) as the tight-frame solution that minimizes $\sum_{i \neq j} G_{ij}^2$ given the capacity constraint.

This progression has two implications. First, superposition is a learned strategy, not a random artifact: the model actively organizes features geometrically to minimize the unified loss. Second, the convergence toward equiangular structure supports the tightness of our capacity bounds (Corollary~\ref{cor:capacity}): the model converges to approximately the optimal packing, consistent with the ETF construction used in the lower bound proof.

\subsection{Feature Dimensionality Transitions}

Figure~\ref{fig:feature_dim_curves} shows feature dimensionality $\|\mathbf{w}_i\|^2$ as a function of feature activation probability $p$ for five features at fixed importance $\approx 0.34$. Several aspects of these curves directly validate the theoretical predictions.

First, features undergo \emph{discontinuous transitions}: dimensionality drops sharply from $\approx 1.3$ to $0$ rather than decreasing smoothly. This is consistent with the sharp phase boundary visible in Figure~\ref{fig:geometric_analysis} and with Corollary~\ref{cor:capacity}: once $p$ exceeds the critical threshold $p^* = \sqrt{1 - d\tau/(n-d)^2}$, the interference cost of maintaining a feature in superposition exceeds the capacity benefit, and the model drops it entirely.

Second, features are dropped in \emph{priority order}: Feature 0 (lowest importance) drops first at $p \approx 0.03$, while higher-importance features persist to larger $p$ values. This is the importance-weighting effect predicted by $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}} = \sum_i p_i(1 - \|\mathbf{w}_i\|^2)^2$: the optimizer maintains features whose contribution to $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}}$ is large, dropping low-importance features when their interference cost becomes prohibitive.

Third, dimensionality values cluster near $\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \approx 1.3 > 1$. Norms exceeding 1.0 reflect the role of the capacity loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cap}}$ pulling features toward unit norm, with the slight excess arising from the importance weighting $\omega_i > 1$.

\subsection{Polysemanticity in a Practical Language Model}

The max-activating token analysis of the $d=256$ bottleneck model reveals that 197 of 256 neurons (77\%) are polysemantic---they activate maximally for tokens from multiple unrelated semantic categories. This polysemanticity rate is consistent with the capacity bound: $n_{\text{eff}} \leq d + O(d^2 S / \tau)$ predicts that at $d=256$ with the sparsity structure of natural language, a large fraction of neurons must share representations across multiple features.

The remaining monosemantic neurons show meaningful specialization: Neuron 1 fires exclusively for \textit{proposal}/\textit{answer} (discourse-structural), Neuron 3 for modal verbs (\textit{will}, \textit{would}), Neuron 5 for \textit{Parliament} (domain-specific), and Neuron 8 for \textit{common}/\textit{single} (quantificational). These monosemantic neurons occupy dedicated dimensions, while the polysemantic majority implement superposition to pack the vocabulary's full feature space into 256 dimensions.

Several patterns emerge from this comparison. First, \textbf{compression ratio governs interference}: across architectures, higher $n/d$ consistently produces higher $\mu_{\max}$ and lower orthogonal fractions, holding for both toy models and practical language models alike. Second, \textbf{Coconut outperforms CT at every $d$}. At matched compression ratios, Coconut achieves 5--14\% more orthogonal pairs and 8--20\% lower $\mu_{\max}$ than CT, an advantage attributable to the iterative reasoning mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:bridging}).

Perhaps more striking is that \textbf{large-$n$ models achieve dramatically better packing}. The translation model ($n=512$, $d=256$) achieves 85.4\% orthogonality at only 2$\times$ compression, far better than the toy model (10.0\% at 2.5$\times$). High-dimensional spaces offer exponentially more nearly-orthogonal directions, consistent with the $n_{\text{eff}} = O(d + d/\sqrt{1-S})$ scaling of Corollary~\ref{cor:capacity}. Taken together, these results demonstrate that \textbf{superposition is universal}: every model with $n > d$ exhibits non-trivial superposition, whether it is a toy autoencoder, a frozen translation model, a GPT-2 transformer, or an iterative reasoning architecture trained on natural language.
